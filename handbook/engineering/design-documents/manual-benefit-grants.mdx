## Summary

Let merchants give a customer a benefit directly, without a subscription or an order behind it.
An expiry date and a note about why are both optional. Today every benefit a customer has is a
side effect of a purchase, and there is no supported way to say "just give this person access".

This adds a `ManualGrant` model as a third owner of benefit grants, alongside subscriptions and
orders. It also adds two API endpoints and a Benefits tab on the customer page in the dashboard.

## Goals

- Grant one or more benefits to a customer from the dashboard or the API, with no purchase.
- Optionally set an expiry. When it passes, the grants are revoked automatically.
- Optionally record a reason, visible to anyone looking at the customer later.
- Revoke a manual grant at any time, per benefit.
- Never disturb grants that came from a purchase. A manual grant and a subscription grant for the
  same benefit must be able to sit side by side and be revoked on their own.

## Non-Goals

- Granting products. This is benefit-level only. Handing someone a full product is a different
  problem (pricing, orders, invoices) and belongs to a free-subscription feature.
- Manual grants for every benefit type in the first version. See
  [Which benefits can be granted](#which-benefits-can-be-granted).
- Editing a manual grant after creation. Change of mind means revoke and re-grant.
- Bulk grants across many customers, or grant templates.

## Why

Support and sales ask for this constantly, and today the workarounds are all bad:

- **Giving a customer something for free.** A refund edge case, a goodwill gesture, an early-access
  user, a conference giveaway. Right now this means creating a fake $0 product or a 100% discount
  code and pushing the customer through a checkout.
- **Fixing a broken grant.** A benefit failed to apply and the merchant wants to re-apply it
  without touching the subscription.
- **Temporary access to a single benefit.** "Give them the Discord role for 30 days" currently
  requires an entire trial product.

All of these are the same shape. This customer should have this benefit, because I said so, maybe
until this date. That shape has no home in the data model, so it gets faked with billing objects,
which then mess up revenue metrics and confuse the customer.

## `ManualGrant` becomes the third scope

```
Subscription ──┐
Order ─────────┼──> BenefitGrant (customer, benefit, member, properties)
ManualGrant ───┘
```

```python
class ManualGrant(RecordModel):
    customer_id: UUID
    reason: str | None
    expires_at: datetime | None
    scheduler_locked_at: datetime | None
```

`BenefitGrant` gains a nullable `manual_grant_id`. The scope composite grows from two columns to
three, and `BenefitGrantScope` / `BenefitGrantScopeArgs` each gain one key.

### Why a scope rather than a flag

A manual grant needs everything a scope gives you. Its own row, so it can sit next to a
subscription grant for the same benefit. Its own lifecycle, so it can expire and be revoked on its
own. And it is the record of where the grant came from, since the reason lives on it. Needing all
three is what being a scope means.

A boolean `is_manual` with `expires_at` and `reason` on `benefit_grants` would be less code, but
it gives up all three. Uniqueness becomes a special case, nothing owns the lifecycle so expiry
needs something of its own to trigger it, and the reason and expiry get written once per benefit
instead of once per decision.

Being a scope also means the existing plumbing carries it. The task actors already take
`**scope: Unpack[BenefitGrantScopeArgs]`, so nothing in the grant and revoke path needs a new
signature. The cost is one extra table and one join.

### One grant, one decision

**A `ManualGrant` is one act of granting.** A merchant picks a customer, ticks three benefits, sets
one expiry, writes one reason. That is one row and three children. Wanting different expiries per
benefit means creating separate manual grants, which is honest, because those are separate
decisions.

**Revoking works per child grant, not per manual grant.** Revoke one benefit and the siblings are
untouched. The parent stays as the record of what happened. Expiry, in contrast, applies to all
remaining children at once. Granting is a decision about a set. Revoking is almost always a fix to
a single benefit.

## Which benefits can be granted

The first version allows `feature_flag`, `custom` and `license_keys`. Everything else is rejected
with a validation error, enforced by one constant (`MANUALLY_GRANTABLE_BENEFIT_TYPES`) mirrored in
the frontend so the picker can't build a request that will be rejected.

This is about keeping the first version small, not a permanent rule. Those three need no extra
thought, since two have no effect outside Polar and license keys already grant and revoke one at a
time. `meter_credit` is the obvious next one and probably the most useful. Adding a type is a
one-line change to the constant plus tests.

The types that reach into someone else's system, Discord roles being the clearest, need a decision
first about what happens when a manual grant and a subscription grant both claim the same role.
That is work to do before adding them rather than a reason they can't be added.

## Lifecycle

### Uniqueness

Two rules, both enforced by the database rather than the service, because a check at create time
races with a second request arriving at the same moment. The existing scope unique index gains
`manual_grant_id`, so a manual grant and a purchase grant for the same benefit are separate rows.
A second unique index, covering only manual grants that are still active, allows at most one per
customer and benefit. Limiting it to active rows means revoking and granting again still works.

Both are added without locking `benefit_grants`, which is too large and too busy to lock. See
ADR-0006.

### Granting

Granting takes a customer, a set of benefits, and the optional expiry and reason. Validation is
all-or-nothing. If any benefit in the request is invalid, nothing is created and the response
lists every problem by position, because partial success would leave the merchant guessing which
of their three benefits landed.

The grants come back pending rather than granted. Applying a benefit can be slow and can fail, so
it runs through the same worker path as every other grant, which means manual grants get the same
retries, error recording and webhooks.

### Revoking

Revoking targets a single grant, and only a manual one. A grant that came from a purchase can't be
revoked this way, since a subscription's grants belong to the subscription.

Revoking is final. Once a manual grant is revoked it stays revoked, even if a grant job for it is
still running. Purchase grants behave the opposite way, because a renewal or a reactivation is
supposed to grant them again, but a manual grant has no renewal, so anything that granted it again
would be a bug.

### Expiry

An expiry is not a scheduled job, it is a column. The scheduler reads `expires_at` off the row to
work out what is due, so there is no job table to keep in sync with the grants themselves and
nothing to clean up when a grant is revoked early. Subscription state changes are already
scheduled this way.

Each manual grant fires once, and the row is claimed as it fires so two schedulers can't both pick
it up. The job then only queues the revokes, one per benefit still active, rather than revoking
anything itself. One benefit failing to revoke does not hold up the rest, and retries stay with
the revoke jobs.

## What comes for free

Because manual grants are ordinary `BenefitGrant` rows with a different owner:

- **Renewal cycles skip them.** Cycling is driven by the subscription, so manual grants are not in
  scope for it. Nothing had to be special-cased, and a test covers it.
- **The customer portal shows them**, with no product attached rather than being hidden. Benefit
  visibility still applies, so a non-public benefit stays hidden.
- **Webhooks and system events fire** as they do for any other grant, so integrations see manual
  grants with no changes.

One thing had to be added, on the API response. `BenefitGrant` gains a nullable `manual_grant`
object (`id`, `reason`, `expires_at`), nested rather than a flat `manual_grant_id`, so a client
sees why a grant exists and when it ends without a second request. `manual_grant !== null` doubles
as the "was this manual" flag.

## API

Both endpoints live on the existing `/v1/benefit-grants` resource rather than a new
`/v1/manual-grants` one. What merchants list and revoke is the grant. The `ManualGrant` is
internal bookkeeping that happens to hold the reason and the expiry, and giving it its own
resource would mean managing two objects to do one thing.

**`POST /v1/benefit-grants/`** returns `201` with the created grants, pending.

```json
{
  "customer_id": "...",
  "benefit_ids": ["...", "..."],
  "expires_at": "2026-09-01T00:00:00Z",
  "reason": "Conference giveaway"
}
```

`expires_at` has to be in the future, `benefit_ids` is capped at 100, and `reason` at 500
characters.

**`DELETE /v1/benefit-grants/{id}`** returns `202` with the grant.

Both require the `benefits:write` scope. Reads stay on `benefits:read`.

## Dashboard

The customer page's old "Benefit Grants" table was a read-only block at the bottom of the Overview
tab. It becomes its own **Benefits** tab, with:

- A **Source** column showing `Manual`, with reason and expiry in a tooltip, or a link to the
  subscription or order that created the grant. A merchant can then see at a glance what is paid
  for and what was given away.
- A **Grant benefits** button opening a modal to pick benefits, optionally set an expiry, and
  optionally write a reason. The picker only offers types that can be granted and hides benefits
  the customer already holds manually, so the merchant can't build a request the API will reject.
- **Revoke** on manual grants only, behind an inline confirm. When the customer would keep access
  through an active purchase anyway, the confirm says so. Otherwise revoking looks worse than it
  is.
- **Polling** while any manual grant is still pending, so rows update to granted or failed without
  a manual refresh.

## Testing

Beyond the usual create and validation coverage, the tests that matter are the ones covering the
rules above. A manual grant and a subscription grant sitting side by side for the same benefit,
including license keys where each grant owns its own key. A grant job not overriding a revoked
manual grant. Manual grants not cycling on renewal. And the scheduler picking the right rows.

## Risks and open questions

- **No record of who granted.** `reason` is free text, and we don't store who did it. Worth adding
  if this is ever used for anything disputed. It is a column on `ManualGrant`, so it is cheap
  later.
- **No notification to the customer, and no warning before expiry.** A manually granted benefit
  appears quietly in their portal and disappears at `expires_at` with no heads-up to either side.
