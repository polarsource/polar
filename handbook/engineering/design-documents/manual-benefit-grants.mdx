## Summary

Let merchants give a customer a benefit directly, without a subscription or an order behind it —
optionally with an expiry date and a note about why. Today every benefit a customer has is a side
effect of a purchase; there is no supported way to say "just give this person access".

This adds a `ManualGrant` entity as a third owner of benefit grants, alongside subscriptions and
orders, plus two API endpoints and a Benefits tab on the customer page in the dashboard.

## Goals

- Grant one or more benefits to a customer from the dashboard or the API, with no purchase.
- Optionally set an expiry; when it passes, the grants are revoked automatically.
- Optionally record a reason, visible to anyone looking at the customer later.
- Revoke a manual grant at any time, per benefit.
- Never disturb grants that came from a purchase — a manual grant and a subscription grant for the
  same benefit must coexist and be revoked independently.

## Non-Goals

- Granting *products*. This is benefit-level only. Handing someone a full product is a different
  problem (pricing, orders, invoices) and belongs to a complimentary-subscription feature.
- Manual grants for every benefit type. Only the types whose grant/revoke work is safe to run per
  grant are allowed — see [Which benefits can be granted](#which-benefits-can-be-granted).
- Editing a manual grant after creation. Change of mind means revoke and re-grant.
- Bulk grants across many customers, or grant templates.

## Why

Support and sales ask for this constantly, and today the workarounds are all bad:

- **Comping a customer** — a refund edge case, a goodwill gesture, an early-access user, a
  conference giveaway. Right now this means creating a fake $0 product or a 100% discount code and
  pushing the customer through a checkout.
- **Fixing a broken grant** — a benefit failed to apply and the merchant wants to re-apply it
  without touching the subscription.
- **Time-boxed access to a single benefit** — "give them the Discord role for 30 days" currently
  requires an entire trial product.

All of these are the same shape: *this customer should have this benefit, because I said so, maybe
until this date*. That shape has no home in the data model, so it gets faked with billing objects,
which then pollute revenue metrics and confuse the customer.

## `ManualGrant` becomes the third scope

```
Subscription ──┐
Order ─────────┼──> BenefitGrant (customer, benefit, member, properties)
ManualGrant ───┘
```

```python
class ManualGrant(RecordModel):
    customer_id: UUID
    reason: str | None
    expires_at: datetime | None
    scheduler_locked_at: datetime | None
```

`BenefitGrant` gains a nullable `manual_grant_id`; the scope composite grows from two columns to
three; `BenefitGrantScope` / `BenefitGrantScopeArgs` each gain one key.

### Why a scope rather than a flag

A manual grant needs everything a scope provides: its own identity (so it can coexist with a
subscription grant for the same benefit), its own lifecycle (expiry, independent revocation), and
it *is* provenance — the reason lives on it. Needing all three is what being a scope means.

The cheaper alternative — a boolean `is_manual` plus `expires_at` and `reason` on `benefit_grants`
— fails on all three: uniqueness stops being "one grant per owner" and becomes a special case in
the index and in every dedupe; a flag drives no lifecycle, so expiry needs a dispatcher invented
from scratch; and the reason and expiry get written once per benefit instead of once per decision.

Being a scope also means the existing plumbing carries it. The actors take
`**scope: Unpack[BenefitGrantScopeArgs]`, so enqueuing
`benefit.grant(customer_id=…, benefit_id=…, manual_grant_id=…)` needs no change to the task
signatures; `resolve_scope` learns one branch and `grant_benefit`/`revoke_benefit` keep their core
logic. The cost is one extra table and one extra join.

### The unit of intent

**A `ManualGrant` is one act of granting.** A merchant picks a customer, ticks three benefits, sets
one expiry, writes one reason. That's one row and three children. Wanting different expiries per
benefit means creating separate manual grants — which is honest, because those are separate
decisions.

**Revocation is per child grant, not per manual grant.** Revoke one benefit and the siblings are
untouched; the parent remains as the record of what happened. Expiry, in contrast, applies to all
remaining children at once. Granting is a decision about a set; revoking is almost always a
correction about a single benefit.

`ManualGrant` has no status column: the state of a manual grant is the state of its children, and a
parent status would drift the moment one child fails and two succeed. It has no `organization_id`
either — authorization walks `ManualGrant → Customer → Organization`, so denormalising the org
would just create a second source of truth.

## Which benefits can be granted

Only three types are allowed:

| Type | Why it's safe |
| --- | --- |
| `feature_flag` | No external side effect. |
| `custom` | No external side effect. |
| `license_keys` | Side effect (the key) is owned by the individual grant — `should_revoke_individually = True`. |

Everything else — Discord roles, GitHub repo invites, downloadables, Slack channels — is rejected
with a validation error, enforced by one constant (`MANUALLY_GRANTABLE_BENEFIT_TYPES`) mirrored in
the frontend so the picker can't build a rejected request.

The reason is coexistence. A manual grant and a subscription grant for the same benefit are two
rows by design, and both can be active at once. That's fine when the side effect belongs to the row
(a license key each) or doesn't exist. It breaks when the side effect is keyed to the *customer*:
there is one Discord role per user, so revoking the manual grant would either strip the role the
subscription is still paying for, or the two grants would fight over the same external state.

The revoke path has a partial defence — it only calls `strategy.revoke()` when
`should_revoke_individually` is set or fewer than two grants remain — but that was written for a
world where both owners are purchases with similar lifetimes. The allowlist can grow per type once
each one's coexistence behaviour has been reasoned through.

## Lifecycle

### Identity — two indexes

**`ix_benefit_grants_scope_unique`** gains `manual_grant_id`, keeping the invariant "one grant per
customer + benefit + member + owner". Every pre-existing row has `manual_grant_id IS NULL`, so
under `NULLS NOT DISTINCT` the collision set for subscription and order grants is unchanged.

**`ix_benefit_grants_active_manual_unique`** is new: a partial unique index on
`(customer_id, benefit_id, member_id)` where `manual_grant_id IS NOT NULL AND revoked_at IS NULL`.
It enforces *at most one active manual grant per customer per benefit*. The service checks for
duplicates before creating, but that check races with a concurrent request; the index is what makes
the rule true. It's partial on `revoked_at IS NULL` so revoke-then-regrant still works — the common
repair flow.

Both index changes build `CONCURRENTLY` in an autocommit block; `benefit_grants` is a large hot
table and cannot be locked. The scope index is rebuilt under a temporary name and then renamed, so
a unique index is enforcing at every moment of the swap. See ADR-0006.

### Granting

`POST /v1/benefit-grants/` takes a customer, benefit IDs, and optional `expires_at` and `reason`.

1. Check the customer is readable by the caller's organization.
2. Resolve the owner member. Manual grants are customer-level, so they call `resolve_member` with
   `member_id=None, is_seat_based=False` — the same path a non-seat purchase takes, which keeps
   grants consistent for orgs that have the member model enabled.
3. Validate every benefit ID: exists, same organization as the customer, grantable type, not
   duplicated in the request, not already manually granted and active.
4. Create the `ManualGrant` and one pending `BenefitGrant` per benefit.
5. Enqueue one `benefit.grant` job per grant, passing `manual_grant_id` as the scope arg.
6. Return the created grants, pending, `201`.

**Validation is all-or-nothing.** Any invalid benefit means nothing is created and the caller gets
a `422` listing every problem, indexed by position in `benefit_ids`. Partial success would leave
the merchant guessing which of their three benefits landed.

**Grants are returned pending, not granted** — the same contract every other grant has, so manual
grants get the same retries, `error` recording, and webhooks.

### Revoking

`DELETE /v1/benefit-grants/{id}` targets a single grant. It refuses grants that came from a
purchase with a `404` — from the caller's point of view there is no manually-granted resource at
that ID. It takes a row lock, returns early if already revoked, otherwise enqueues `benefit.revoke`.
Returns `202`.

**Revocation is terminal for manual grants.** Once a manual grant's child is revoked, a racing
`benefit.grant` job must not resurrect it, so `grant_benefit` returns early when it finds an
already-revoked manual-scoped grant instead of re-applying:

```python
elif grant.is_revoked and scope.get("manual_grant") is not None:
    return grant
```

The same early return would be wrong for subscription and order grants, which are legitimately
re-granted on renewal or reactivation. Manual grants have no renewal concept, so nothing would ever
legitimately re-grant them.

Grant and revoke jobs also take a `FOR UPDATE` row lock when the scope is a manual grant, so a
concurrent grant and revoke serialize instead of interleaving.

### Expiry

**Decision: an APScheduler job store, not a periodic sweep.** Polar already schedules subscription
state changes this way (`_SubscriptionScheduleJobStore`), so `ManualGrantExpiryJobStore` copies that
shape: the scheduler asks the database "what's due?" directly instead of us maintaining a table of
scheduled jobs. Nothing has to be kept in sync — set `expires_at` and the row *is* the schedule —
whereas a cron sweep would either poll constantly or expire grants late.

When a grant comes due, the store dispatches it under an atomic claim:

```sql
UPDATE manual_grants
   SET scheduler_locked_at = now()
 WHERE id = :id AND scheduler_locked_at IS NULL
```

Zero rows updated means another scheduler already claimed it; only the winner sends the Dramatiq
message.

**The claim is never cleared.** Expiry is one-shot, so a locked row is a dispatched row and it
permanently leaves the scheduling query — simpler than the subscription store, which has to re-arm.
Retries after dispatch belong to the revoke jobs, which have their own retry semantics;
re-dispatching the parent would just enqueue duplicate revokes.

The scheduling query is backed by a partial index on `expires_at` covering exactly the rows the
scheduler cares about. Job-store failures are reported to Sentry before re-raising, because
APScheduler otherwise swallows them into a warning and retries — a broken store would look like
nothing happening at all.

The expiry job does no revoking itself: it loads the manual grant and enqueues one `benefit.revoke`
per still-active child, so one benefit failing to revoke doesn't block the others.

## What comes for free

Because manual grants are ordinary `BenefitGrant` rows with a different owner:

- **Renewal cycles skip them.** `enqueue_benefit_grant_cycles(subscription=…)` lists grants by
  scope, so manual grants simply aren't in the query. No exclusion logic; a regression test pins it.
- **The customer portal shows them.** Its subscription/order/product joins are outer joins, so a
  manual grant appears with no product attached rather than being filtered out. (Benefit visibility
  still applies — a non-public benefit stays hidden.)
- **Webhooks and system events fire** from `grant_benefit`/`revoke_benefit`, so integrations see
  manual grants with no changes.

The one addition is provenance on the read model: `BenefitGrant` gains a nullable `manual_grant`
object (`id`, `reason`, `expires_at`), nested rather than a flat `manual_grant_id`, so a client
sees why a grant exists and when it ends without a second request. `manual_grant !== null` is also
the "was this manual" flag.

## API surface

Both endpoints live on the existing `/v1/benefit-grants` resource rather than a new
`/v1/manual-grants` one. What merchants list and revoke is the **grant**; the `ManualGrant` is
internal bookkeeping that happens to hold the reason and the expiry. Exposing it as its own
resource would mean managing two objects to do one thing.

**`POST /v1/benefit-grants/`** → `201`, the created grants (pending).

```json
{
  "customer_id": "...",
  "benefit_ids": ["...", "..."],
  "expires_at": "2026-09-01T00:00:00Z",
  "reason": "Conference giveaway"
}
```

`expires_at` is validated as a future datetime, `benefit_ids` is capped at 100, `reason` at 500
characters.

**`DELETE /v1/benefit-grants/{id}`** → `202`, the grant.

Both require the `benefits:write` scope; reads stay on `benefits:read`.

## Dashboard

The customer page's old "Benefit Grants" table was a read-only block at the bottom of the Overview
tab. It becomes its own **Benefits** tab, with:

- A **Source** column: `Manual` (reason and expiry in a tooltip), or a link to the subscription or
  order that produced the grant — so a merchant can see at a glance what's paid for and what was
  given away.
- A **Grant benefits** button opening a modal: pick benefits, optionally set an expiry, optionally
  write a reason. The picker only offers grantable types and hides benefits the customer already
  holds manually, so the merchant can't build a request the API will reject.
- **Revoke** on manual grants only, behind an inline confirm. When the customer would keep access
  through an active purchase anyway, the confirm says so — otherwise revoking looks more
  destructive than it is.
- **Polling** while any manual grant is still pending, so rows settle to granted or failed without
  a manual refresh.

## Testing

Beyond the usual create/validation coverage, the tests that matter are the ones pinning the
invariants above: a manual grant and a subscription grant coexisting for the same benefit
(including license keys, where each grant owns its own key), a grant job not overriding a revoked
manual grant, manual grants not cycling on renewal, and the scheduler's eligibility query.

## Risks and open questions

- **The allowlist will be challenged.** Merchants will want to manually grant Discord roles. That
  needs per-type coexistence design — deciding what happens when two owners claim one external
  role — not just an addition to the constant.
- **No record of who granted.** `reason` is free text; we don't store the acting user. Worth adding
  if this is ever used for anything contested. It's a column on `ManualGrant`, so it's cheap later.
- **No notification to the customer**, and **no warning before expiry**. A manually granted benefit
  appears silently in their portal and disappears at `expires_at` with no heads-up to either side.
