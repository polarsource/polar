## Summary

Create orders without stamping an `order_item_id` onto every billing entry, so that a
subscription with a very large number of entries can be invoiced without settlement time scaling
with that count.

A **meter period** is one row per (subscription, meter, period) carrying a time window.
Settlement aggregates the billing entries whose timestamps fall inside it, prices the result,
and records what it charged on the period row.

Two rows are written per settlement — the period and its order item — whatever the subscription's
volume. Scope is a timestamp range fixed when the period opens, so it never changes and never
needs to be marked. `billed_amount` on the row is a high-water mark, so a period can be settled
more than once and charges only the increase.

Billing entries are untouched: still one per event, still written at ingestion, still carrying
the customer-to-subscription resolution decided there. This changes only what an invoice
considers in scope.

One table, three operations, behind a per-organization switch.

## Background

An invoice's metered scope is `order_item_id IS NULL` — the entries not yet claimed by an order.
Settling therefore has to *claim* them, stamping an id onto every row.

Two things follow from scope being a column that invoicing writes.

**Settling is proportional to the entry count, so any fixed time limit binds eventually.**
`order_item_id` is indexed, so the update cannot be HOT and every row writes into every index on
the table; and each row must be visited. Measured in production on 2026-08-17, a 624k-entry
settlement failed 16 consecutive times against a 60s task limit, each rolled-back attempt leaving
dead tuples that slowed the next. [#13785](https://github.com/polarsource/polar/pull/13785) has
since removed the quadratic part of that cost and raised the limit to 600s, and the same
settlement now completes in 186s. The work still scales with the entry count — see
[Evidence](#evidence).

**An invoice changes what a later invoice can see.** This produced the enterprise seat overcharge
(`polar-internal#113`): a period's allowance arrived as an event carrying its own entry, the
first invoice of the period claimed it, and every later invoice saw usage with no credit.
[#13711](https://github.com/polarsource/polar/pull/13711) fixed the incident by not billing
metered usage on a plan change at all — removing the second settlement rather than making it
safe. The property is unchanged.

A third thing is missing rather than wrong: **no row represents a period.**
`Subscription.current_meter_period_start/end` is two columns the scheduler reads to decide when
to wake, carrying no record of what has already been billed. A `max` meter settled twice bills
its peak twice (12, then 15, for a period peak of 15).

Note which mechanism fails. `billing_entry` is 6.9 GB against 188 GB of events, and the ingest
fan-out runs at ~5 events/s against a ceiling in the hundreds — so *creating* entries is not
what breaks. *Claiming* them is. Both of those hold at today's volume and are revisited under
[Deferred: rollups](#deferred-rollups); neither is a reason to think the entry table is free
forever.

## Goals

- **Settlement cost stops scaling with the number of billing entries.** It is the one cost that
  grows without bound as usage grows.
- An invoice's scope is immutable, so invoicing cannot change what a later invoice sees.
- A past invoice can be recomputed at any time.
- One grouping rule for every aggregation type: a period is per meter, so there is no
  summable/non-summable split and no per-price deduplication.

## Non-Goals

- Changing the pricing model (`ProductPriceMeteredUnit`, cap amounts) or the meter definition
  (filter, aggregation, unit).
- Changing event ingestion, the `events` table, or how billing entries are created. Entries
  keep being written at ingestion, which is what makes them the right thing to aggregate.
- Reducing the size of `billing_entry`, or removing the per-meter ingest fan-out. Both were
  candidates; the measurements below say neither is the binding constraint.
- Cash balances. See [wallets](/engineering/design-documents/wallets).
- Building pre-aggregation infrastructure now. See [Deferred: rollups](#deferred-rollups).

## Design

### One table

```python
class MeterPeriod(RecordModel):
    """One accrual window for a (subscription, meter) pair."""
    __tablename__ = "meter_periods"

    subscription_id: Mapped[UUID]
    meter_id: Mapped[UUID]
    product_price_id: Mapped[UUID]      # the price this period bills at

    starts_at: Mapped[datetime]         # half-open window [starts_at, ends_at)
    ends_at: Mapped[datetime]
    status: Mapped[MeterPeriodStatus]   # accruing | settled
    billable: Mapped[bool]              # False during trial

    quantity: Mapped[Decimal]           # as of the last settlement
    credited_units: Mapped[Decimal]     # as of the last settlement
    billed_amount: Mapped[int]          # total charged for this period so far
    currency: Mapped[str]

    __table_args__ = (UniqueConstraint("subscription_id", "meter_id", "starts_at"),)
```

### Three operations

**Open** — at subscription start, cycle, or a meter-interval boundary, insert one period per
metered price on the subscription. The price is snapshotted onto the row. Idempotent: a price
that already has an accruing period is left alone.

**Read** — current usage is the meter's aggregation over the entries in `[starts_at, now)`.
Scoping on `BillingEntry.subscription_id` rather than on a customer is what carries the
ingest-time resolution: a seat member's usage was already attributed to the seat holder's
subscription when its entry was written, and each event produces exactly one entry against
exactly one subscription, so a customer holding two subscriptions on one meter cannot be billed
by both.

**Settle** — compute what the window owes, then charge the increase over what has already been
billed:

```python
quantity = aggregate(meter, window=[period.starts_at, cutoff))
credits  = sum(credit.units for credit in credits_in_scope(period))
amount   = price.get_amount(quantity - credits)

charge = amount - period.billed_amount     # == amount on a first settlement
```

Settling is split: `compute_settlement` is read-only, `apply_settlement` persists. The charge
preview and `subscription.update_meters` both build line items through the same generator, so a
settlement that wrote from inside it would let previewing a charge advance a period's billing
state.

`apply_settlement(close=False)` records the settlement and leaves the window accruing with its
credits in scope. Two properties follow: an allowance is scoped to the period rather than to a
billing entry, so settling cannot consume it; and `billed_amount` is a high-water mark, so a
`max` meter settled twice charges `price(15) - price(12)` rather than `price(12) + price(15)`.

Nothing reaches that path today — since #13711 every settlement from the order path closes its
period. It is kept because it makes settling twice *safe* rather than merely unused: #12510
settles on its own clock, and every non-additive pricing model (tiered, graduated, package)
double-bills without it.

### Windows and late events

Period membership is decided on `Event.timestamp`, never `ingested_at`; `ingested_at` stays
operational, for detecting late arrivals.

**A window is always settled before the last of its entries exists.** Entries are written by a
cron behind a watermark, so at any cutoff the most recent events have been ingested but not yet
turned into entries — measured at 12–20 minutes of lag. The sweep needs nothing for this: those
entries are simply still pending and the next invoice picks them up. A window has to recover
them explicitly.

`compute_corrections` does that by rechecking the period settled at the previous invoice: the
period row already records what was billed, so recomputing its window and charging the increase
over `billed_amount` recovers whatever arrived late. The resulting line carries that period's
dates, so the customer sees which window it belongs to.

It is bounded to that one period per meter because an invoice is the only thing that runs it —
anything older has already had its chance. A duration-based bound cannot work here: it would
have to exceed one billing interval to fire at all.

Only an increase is billed. A decrease is possible for `min` and `avg` meters, and is not
refunded.

**A cancellation settles late on purpose.** There is no next invoice to correct onto, so the
final order is enqueued with an hour's delay — comfortably past the 15-minute cron and the
12–20 minutes of watermark lag. Because the cutoff is passed explicitly,
waiting changes when the window is computed, not which window, so the customer is billed for the
same period either way. The sweep gains this too: entries created in those minutes used to sit
pending on a cancelled subscription forever.

Usage arriving later than that is still lost — nothing bounded can catch a customer who sends
last month's events tomorrow — but it is now an unusual case rather than every cancellation.

## What this changes

| Today | With periods |
|---|---|
| scope is `order_item_id IS NULL` | scope is `start_timestamp` inside the period's window |
| settling stamps an id onto every entry | settling writes the period row and the order item |
| `_link_pending`, `link_pending_by_subscription_and_price` / `_and_meter` | — |
| `PendingByPrice` / `PendingByMeter`, `processed_meters`, the active-price scan | the meter is a column on the period |
| `_get_metered_line_item` and `_get_metered_line_item_by_meter` | one function |
| nowhere to record what a period has been billed | `billed_amount`, a high-water mark |

Entries themselves are untouched: still one per event, still written at ingestion, still
carrying the customer-to-subscription resolution decided at that moment. That resolution —
direct subscription over seat holder, the trial boundary, the price in force — is subtle,
already correct, and inherited for free by aggregating over entries rather than re-deriving it
from events at settlement.

The summable/non-summable split disappears: one window, one aggregate per period, nothing to
group by price and nothing to deduplicate. `get_quantity` still branches on `count`, which
counts entries directly rather than reaching through to `events` — a performance shortcut the
sweep already carries, not a difference in meaning.

## Evidence

Measured on production, 2026-08-17, against subscription `84234af1` — 624k pending entries on a
single `count` meter, whose settlement had failed 16 consecutive times on the 60s task limit.

**Settling costs a minute of reads before it writes anything.** The sweep stamps `order_item_id`
onto every entry, in batches of 5,000. `order_item_id` is indexed, so the update cannot be HOT
and every row writes into all **8 indexes** — ~5M index insertions for one settlement. Worse,
the rows for one subscription sit ~1.6 per page *in index order*, so visiting them reads ~3 GB
of scattered heap at the ~52 MB/s the storage sustains on random access. That is ~71s before a
single write, against a 60s limit.

**Then it finished, in 186 seconds.** [#13784](https://github.com/polarsource/polar/pull/13784)
and [#13785](https://github.com/polarsource/polar/pull/13785) added
`ix_billing_entry_pending_link`, keyset-paginated the loop so a batch resumes at `id > last`
rather than rebuilding a bitmap over the whole subscription, and raised the actor's `time_limit`
from 60s to 600s. On 2026-08-18 the same settlement completed in **186s**.

That removes the quadratic term the six-second batches were spending their time in, and it ends
the incident. It does not change the shape of the cost:

| | 624k entries | scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Stamp every entry, pre-#13785 | never completed | quadratic in the batch count |
| Stamp every entry, today | **186s** | linear — ~3.4k entries/s |
| Count the window (what a period settles) | **1,127 ms**, cold cache | flat — two row writes per meter |

186s is 31% of the raised ceiling, so this subscription has roughly **3× headroom** before
600s is not enough either, on a table where the per-row cost only rises: a non-HOT update writes
every index, and `billing_entry` now carries ten, two more than when the 71s figure was measured.
A 186s transaction also holds back vacuum and pushes replication lag for its duration.

So the case for this design is no longer "settlement cannot finish". It is that settlement costs
186 seconds and grows with usage, against ~1 second that does not.

The window scan reads 624,893 rows across 36,281 heap blocks — **17 rows per block**, in
physical order, at ~367 MB/s. Same rows, same table; the difference is entirely the access
pattern. `ix_billing_entry_window` narrows it further: one range scan instead of a `BitmapAnd`
of two ill-suited indexes, and `count(*)` needs no heap columns so it can go index-only.

**The trap is structural.** `order_item_id` must be indexed to find pending entries — it is the
most-scanned index on the table, 28.9M scans and 8.2B tuples read. Being indexed is exactly what
makes writing it expensive. The column that defines billing scope has to be queryable *and*
has to be written on every settlement, and those requirements fight each other in proportion to
entry count.

A period's scope is a timestamp range, written once when the period opens and never updated.
There is nothing to stamp, so none of the above applies.

Two things worth fixing regardless of this design, since the sweep will exist for a while. The
planner's estimate of 3,529 rows against 664,992 sent it to a bitmap plan that rebuilt the full
bitmap on every batch; #13784 and #13785 address this with a dedicated index and keyset
pagination, and extended statistics on `(subscription_id, product_price_id)` would fix the
estimate itself. Autovacuum still wants tuning: the default `0.2` scale factor let 2.75M dead
tuples accumulate on a 16M-row table before triggering, and each rolled-back settlement attempt
adds more.

### Deferred: rollups

Settling a period is one aggregation over every event in it. At high enough volume the answer is
an incremental rollup keyed `(customer_id, meter_id, bucket_start)`, upserted per ingest batch,
so settlement reads ~720 hourly buckets instead of N events.

**Not built.** The period model is what makes rollups possible at all — pre-aggregation is only
sound over immutable, closed windows, which the per-event pending set is not.

It is also where the *source* of a period's quantity gets swapped. Settlement reads events
through their billing entries today, which inherits the ingest-time resolution and is what makes
this change small. At 10-100× the volume the entry table and the per-meter fan-out both become
binding, and quantity moves to a rollup keyed on the window instead. The period row, its window
and its high-water mark are unchanged by that — only `get_quantity` is. Treat that method as the
seam.

When we do: `count`, `sum`, `min` and `max` merge across buckets trivially and `avg` decomposes
into `(sum, count)`, but `unique` does not merge exactly without keeping the underlying sets.
`get_quantities` already carves `avg` and `unique` out of its optimised path, so that constraint
is pre-existing.

## Security considerations

**Data stored.** `meter_periods` holds a window, an aggregate quantity, credited units, and what
has been charged, keyed by subscription, meter and price. No customer-identifying data and no
event payloads: the quantity is a number derived at settlement, and the events it came from stay
where they are. It is billing state at the same trust tier as `order_item`, and inherits the
organization scoping of the subscription it hangs off.

**Trust boundary.** The window is the boundary. `[starts_at, ends_at)` is fixed when the period
opens and never updated, so an invoice cannot change what a later invoice sees. That is the
property whose absence produced the enterprise seat overcharge (`polar-internal#113`), where the
first invoice of a period consumed the allowance entry and every later one billed usage with no
credit against it.

**Attribution.** Quantity is scoped on `BillingEntry.subscription_id`, so a period only ever sees
usage that ingestion already attributed to that subscription — seat member to seat holder, the
trial boundary, the price in force. Each event produces exactly one entry against exactly one
subscription, so a customer holding two subscriptions on one meter cannot be billed by both.
Re-deriving attribution from events at settlement is what would reopen that.

**Exposure.** No public API reads or writes periods. The switch is staff-only, absent from
`OrganizationFeatureSettingsUpdate`, and settable from the backoffice; opening a period is a
backoffice action. Nothing here widens what an organization can see about another.

**The integrity risk is reversal, not access.** Turning the switch off after a period has settled
double-bills: those entries were billed but never linked, so the sweep finds them pending and
charges them again. Nothing in the schema prevents the flag being unset — the backoffice renders
it as an ordinary checkbox — so it is a procedural constraint today. Reversing it safely means
marking the affected entries first.

## Rollout

The switch is `feature_settings.metered_billing_periods_enabled`, staff-managed (absent from
`OrganizationFeatureSettingsUpdate`) and off for every organization.

**A period covering the window selects the path, not the flag.** An organization switched on
mid-period has no period for the window it is already in, so billing falls back to the sweep
until the next cycle opens one. That fallback fires *only* while the subscription has never had
a period: afterwards its entries are billed by window and deliberately left unlinked, so a sweep
would bill every one of them again.

Turning it on:

1. Flip the flag. Nothing changes immediately — the current window still bills by sweep, and
   entries keep being written exactly as before.
2. At the next cycle, `reset_meters` opens the first period.
3. That cycle's invoice is the last one to link entries. Every invoice after it settles a
   window and links nothing.

**Turning it off is not safe** once a period has settled. The entries in that window were billed
but never linked, so the sweep would find them pending and bill them a second time. Treat the
switch as one-way per organization; reversing it needs those entries marked first.

### Built

`MeterPeriod`, its repository and service, migrations `6702e350fa99` (the table) and
`8f31c4d7be05` (`ix_billing_entry_window`, built `CONCURRENTLY` — `billing_entry` is ~16M rows
and a blocking build would stall every write to it for minutes), and the switch.

Both halves of the billing path reach it through `compute_pending_metered_line_items`, which is
the single producer of metered line items.

That index is charged to the sweep: it takes `billing_entry` to ten indexes, so every stamped
row does about a tenth more index work for as long as anything is still sweeping.

Shadow-running both paths and comparing was considered and dropped in favour of the switch —
which trades early detection for simplicity, so the first switched-on organization should be
small and its first two invoices checked by hand.

### Not built

- Settling on a period's own clock. Settlement still happens when an invoice is built, which is
  what bounds the correction look-back to one period.
- Anything that would let the switch be reversed.
- Opening periods for many subscriptions at once. The backoffice action is per subscription,
  which suits switching one organization on deliberately and would not suit a fleet.

### Relationship to #12510

[#12510](https://github.com/polarsource/polar/pull/12510) settles meter cycles independently of
billing, and this design adopts its semantics rather than reopening them: a two-clock scheduler
wakes on whichever of `current_period_end` / `current_meter_period_end` fires first; each meter
boundary settles into its own `subscription_meter_cycle` order and re-grants credits; a boundary
coinciding with the billing boundary folds into the renewal invoice. Under periods those two
clocks become one — the next `MeterPeriod.ends_at` — since a billing period is just the case
where the meter interval equals the billing interval.

Both halves of the meter cycle reach the period path without extra wiring:
`compute_pending_metered_line_items` is the single producer of metered line items, so the
`subscription_meter_cycle` order settles periods, and `cycle_meters` calls `reset_meters`, which
opens the next one. Its tests (`tests/e2e/lifecycle/test_meter_cycle.py`) are the contract a
switched-on organization has to keep meeting — they caught two `lazy="raise"` failures in the
switch's flag checks that no unit test did.

## Known issues in the current model

The switch is off everywhere, so these still need fixing independently:

1. **Settlement cost on the largest subscriptions.** No longer failing: after #13784 and #13785,
   `84234af1` settled its 624k pending entries in 186s against a 600s limit. What remains is the
   scaling — 186s of linear work per cycle, with ~3× headroom before the raised limit binds too.
   **This design addresses it, but not by flipping the flag alone** — a subscription switched on
   mid-life has no period covering the window being settled, and the fallback correctly sends it
   back to the sweep. It needs a period opened over that window first, from the backoffice
   (*Open Meter Period* on the subscription page), after which settlement is a ~1s window count
   and two row writes per meter. The entries then stay unlinked permanently, which is why the
   switch is one-way for that organization.

2. **Pending entries on canceled subscriptions** — 41% of the pending set, the oldest 8 months
   past its period end. Needs a root cause before cleanup.
3. **`subscription.update_meters` recomputes from scratch on every 500-event batch**, and it
   exists to keep `subscription_meter` fresh for dashboard reads rather than for billing. Its
   cost therefore grows with a subscription's pending set, on a schedule set by ingest volume. A
   debounce will not help: batches already arrive tens of minutes apart, so nothing coalesces.
   It needs to be incremental or computed on read.
4. **The line-item label is wrong.** `get_amount_and_label` renders `units - credited_units` as
   "N consumed units", so a customer sees their net as their consumption. Independent of
   everything above.
