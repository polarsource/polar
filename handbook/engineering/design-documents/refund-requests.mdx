## Briefing

EU consumer law (Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU) gives B2C buyers a 14-day
**right of withdrawal** on distance contracts. For digital goods this right can be
waived — but only under strict conditions. Today a customer who wants their money
back has to email the merchant; Polar gives them no path at all.

So customers need a way to **request a refund** from the portal. The fear is the
opposite failure mode: a naive "customer clicks → auto-refund" flow lets anyone
download the product and then claim their money back, getting the digital good
for free. So the design has to do two things at once: **comply with the law**
without **opening a free-product loophole**.

The resolution is to separate the two halves. **Every customer can always
request** — asking is never gated. Polar routes the request to the merchant with
the legal position attached: *"this customer is in Spain, they accepted the
waiver, but they have not downloaded the files — you have to refund."* The
merchant decides. Polar advises.

This document maps what exists today, the legal principle behind that advice,
and a phased plan.

## Goals

- Any customer can open a **refund request** from the portal, recorded as a
  support case (audit trail), on every path.
- The merchant sees, on the case, **what the law requires** for that specific
  order — jurisdiction, waiver state, access state, days elapsed.
- Merchants are protected from abuse via a **waiver opt-in** captured at checkout
  ("immediate access ⇒ you waive your 14-day withdrawal right").
- Refunding pulls back **every benefit granted through the order**.
- Merchants get **signals** (refund history per customer, notes, optional
  blocklist) to manage repeat abusers.

## Non-goals (for v1)

- Automatic, no-merchant-in-the-loop refunds for arbitrary products.
- **Polar enforcing the refund on the merchant's behalf.** As merchant of record
  Polar arguably could, but v1 informs and lets the merchant act.
- Gating *who may ask*. Eligibility shapes the advice, never the button.
- Access semantics for **custom** and **feature flag** benefits, where Polar has
  no signal it can assert (see below). They stay out of the eligibility read.
- A full customer reputation/risk-scoring engine.

## The legal principle (this is the whole design)

Under the Directive, for digital content the merchant **loses the obligation to
refund** only if **all three** hold:

1. The consumer gave **prior express consent** to begin access before the 14 days
   elapse, **and**
2. The consumer **acknowledged they thereby lose** the right of withdrawal, **and**
3. The merchant **confirmed** that consent on a durable medium.

That is exactly the "waiver opt-in upon purchase." It produces **two compliant
regimes**, and the loophole only appears if you mix them:

| Regime | Condition | What Polar tells the merchant | Loophole? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Waiver path** | Waiver given → immediate access granted | Once **accessed**: your call | No — they waived |
| **No-waiver path** | No waiver | Within 14 days: **you must refund**, *regardless of access* | Only if you granted access anyway |

> **The one rule that closes the loophole:**
> **Never grant access without either a recorded waiver or accepting the 14-day refund liability.**
> i.e. "Have it now if you waive, or wait 14 days."

Everything below exists to make that rule enforceable — and to put the right
sentence in front of the merchant when a request lands.

**Takeaway:** the refund engine, the case primitive, **and the grant/revoke
lifecycle** are already done — every benefit type records `granted_at` /
`revoked_at` and is revocable. The genuinely missing pieces are narrower:
**consent capture** at checkout, **order-scoped revocation** (today it's
all-or-nothing per customer), and the **customer-facing case UI**.

## Plan

Because the request is never gated, the button is **not** blocked on the legal
read. Phase 1 ships a working request flow on its own; Phase 2 makes the merchant's
decision an informed one.

### Phase 0 — Foundations

1. **Consent / waiver capture at checkout.**
   - Detect EU B2C (`is_business_customer` + billing country).
   - Waiver opt-in: *"Give me immediate access. I acknowledge I lose my 14-day
     withdrawal right once I access the content."*
   - Store the consent **with its text version + timestamp** on the order —
     durable-medium proof is itself a legal requirement, not just nice-to-have.

2. **Unify an `accessed` predicate per benefit grant.** The grant already carries
   `granted_at` + external identity in `properties`; this just normalizes *what
   counts as access* per type:
   - **Consumption-style** (downloadables, license keys, meter credits): access =
     the discrete event (download / activation / units consumed), tracked
     separately from the grant.
   - **Provisioned-access** (Discord, GitHub, Slack): the grant *is* the
     supply-begun event — `granted_at` is the access signal, and revoke is real.
   - **Custom and feature flag**: fulfillment is merchant-defined and both
     strategies are grant/revoke no-ops, so `granted_at` is the only thing Polar
     can assert — and it asserts almost nothing. A custom benefit may be a
     thank-you note the customer sees the moment checkout completes; calling that
     "accessed" and dropping the refund obligation would be wrong. **These two
     types are excluded from the access read in v1.** Polar tells the merchant it
     cannot see whether the customer used their purchase.

3. **Order-scoped benefit revocation** (merchant endpoint) so a refund pulls back
   everything granted through that order, and nothing the customer owns from
   another one.

### Phase 1 — The refund request

4. Implement the `refund_request` case type, mirroring `dispute_case.py`: a
   `RefundRequestSupportCase` subclass with a FK to `order`, plus a handler service.
5. Build the **customer-facing case UI** (net-new portal surface). This also
   unblocks customer-visible disputes later — the `customer` audience already
   exists in the model.
6. Put the **request button** on the order in the portal, available to every
   customer. It opens a `refund_request` case; it does not move money. The
   merchant resolves the case, and approving runs the existing refund plus the
   Phase 0.3 order-scoped revoke — still subject to `organization.can_refund` and
   `order.refunds_blocked`, which already guard `refund/service.py:219`.
   - For a subscription order, the request carries an explicit customer choice:
     **"Cancel my subscription so I am not charged again."** A refund does not
     imply cancellation by default, but someone invoking withdrawal usually wants
     both — so ask rather than infer.

### Phase 2 — The advisory layer

7. **Legal read** on the case, computed from jurisdiction, waiver state, access
   state and days elapsed: `must_refund = EU_B2C AND within_14_days AND NOT
   (waived AND accessed)`. Custom and feature flag benefits report *unknown*
   access rather than a value.
8. Surface it to the merchant **as a sentence on the case**, not as a gate:
   *"Spain, B2C. Waiver accepted at checkout. No files downloaded. Within 14
   days — you are required to refund."* The merchant still clicks the button.
   Whether Polar should ever enforce this itself, as merchant of record, is left
   open below.

### Phase 3 — Abuse controls

9. **Per-customer refund metrics** — derivable today by filtering the refund
   table on `customer_id`; surface "N refund requests across your products."
10. **Customer notes** (net-new field).
11. **Email / card blocklist** — net-new, or lean on Stripe Radar + the existing
    `fraudulent` refund reason (which already warns it blocks customers).

## Always a case

Every customer-initiated refund goes through a `refund_request` case, including
the ones a merchant approves in one click. It's cheap — the primitive exists —
and the case history *is* the per-customer abuse signal Phase 3 needs. If a later
version does add auto-approval for some merchants, it still writes the case and
closes it; the trail survives the shortcut.

## Settled

- **Asking is never gated.** Every customer can open a request. Eligibility
  shapes what Polar tells the merchant, not who gets a button.
- **The 14-day clock starts at order paid** — conclusion of the contract.
- **Revocation is order-scoped**, not per-benefit.
- **Custom and feature flag benefits sit out the access read** in v1. Polar tells
  the merchant it has no signal.
- **The button is not merchant-toggleable.** A merchant cannot opt out of the law,
  and cannot opt out of being asked.
- **Refund does not imply cancellation.** The customer says so explicitly.

## Open questions

<OpenQuestion>
  Should Polar ever **enforce** the refund rather than advise it? As merchant of
  record Polar is the legal seller, so a strict reading says it can. v1 advises.
  What would make us change that — repeat non-compliance by a merchant, a
  regulator complaint, something else?
</OpenQuestion>

<OpenQuestion>
  **Does the waiver belong on subscriptions at all?** The withdrawal-right waiver
  is written for digital *content* supplied in one go. A subscription is an
  ongoing *service*, which is never "fully delivered on access" and carries its
  own partial-refund rules for the part already consumed. Collecting the same
  waiver on both may be wrong. Needs a legal answer before Phase 0 ships the
  checkbox.
</OpenQuestion>

<OpenQuestion>
  Related: should a withdrawal on a subscription produce a **prorated** refund for
  the unused part of the period rather than a full one? Out of scope for v1, but
  it changes what the request UI asks for.
</OpenQuestion>
