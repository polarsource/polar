## Briefing

Polar sells to the customer. Our terms say it plainly — *"You appoint Polar as
your non-exclusive reseller of the Product"*, *"Polar is the seller of the Product
to Buyer."* The merchant sells to Polar; Polar sells to the buyer. Under the
Consumer Rights Directive the party who contracts with the consumer is the
**trader**, and the trader is the one who must give the money back. That is Polar,
not the merchant. A merchant refusing a refund does not discharge Polar's duty.

Two things follow, and they set the whole design.

**We owe a withdrawal button, and we are late.** Directive (EU) 2023/2673 added
Article 11a to the CRD: any trader letting consumers conclude contracts through an
online interface must show a withdrawal function. It applies from **19 June 2026**.
We do not have one. Non-compliance carries fines up to 4% of annual turnover, and —
worse in practice — stretches the customer's 14-day window to **12 months and 14
days**.

**Where the right survives, Polar refunds.** Not "Polar advises and the merchant
decides." Our terms already carry the mechanism: Polar may cancel a transaction and
refund the buyer, and §10.5 makes the supplier reimburse Polar. The debit lands on
the merchant's balance the way a chargeback already does.

This document sets out how we close that gap without holding benefits back for two
weeks, and without handing out free product.

## Goals

- Ship an **Article 11a withdrawal function** that meets the spec.
- Capture a **waiver at checkout**, so an accessed one-time purchase stops being
  refundable on demand.
- Keep **immediate access** on every order. No delayed granting.
- Refund **without asking the merchant** where the obligation is Polar's.
- Record every request as a support case, for audit and for abuse signals.

## Non-goals (for v1)

- **Holding benefits back for 14 days.** The mandatory waiver removes the need;
  see the bet below.
- Access semantics for **custom** and **feature flag** benefits, where Polar has
  no signal it can assert. They stay out of the access read.
- A full customer reputation/risk-scoring engine.

## The bet

The waiver checkbox is **mandatory**. You cannot complete checkout without it.

This is a deliberate risk and the reviewers should weigh it as one. The upside is
large: a mandatory waiver is what lets us grant access immediately on every order
while still closing the free-product hole. Without it we would have to withhold
benefits for 14 days on any unwaived purchase — the automatic-granting problem —
which is the most expensive thing in this whole design.

The risk is that a checkbox you cannot decline is a weak choice, and the exception
is read strictly. If a court or regulator voids it, the waiver falls away, accessed
customers get their right back, and we owe refunds on the exact orders it was meant
to protect. We would also have told those customers they had no right, which is its
own exposure and is the kind of failure that triggers the 12-month window.

**Fallback if counsel says no:** make the tick optional and grant immediately
anyway. Legally clean, still no hold-back, but anyone who declines gets 14 days of
free product. We would take that trade rather than build delayed granting.

## The rules

Under the Directive the withdrawal right on digital content survives unless **all
three** hold: the consumer gave prior express consent to begin, acknowledged they
lose the right, and the trader confirmed it on a durable medium. Critically, the
right also survives until **supply has actually begun** — the tick alone never
extinguishes it.

That gives three cases, and only one of them involves the merchant.

| Case | Who decides | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **One-time, waived, accessed** | The merchant | Right is gone. A refund is goodwill. Opens a case; merchant resolves it. |
| **One-time, waived, not accessed** | Nobody | Right survives. Polar refunds automatically. Nothing to revoke. |
| **Subscription** | Nobody | Waiver has no effect (see below). Polar refunds with a pro-rata deduction. |

The second case is nearly free to honour: no benefit was consumed, so there is
nothing to claw back. Refund the money, close the case.

### Subscriptions are different

In **Sky Österreich** (C-234/25, judgment of 9 July 2026) the CJEU held that a
subscription of a dynamic nature — continuously updated, watching user behaviour,
recommending on that basis — is a digital **service**, not digital **content**. The
Article 16(m) waiver only covers content. So for those subscriptions the right
*"cannot be lost simply because the consumer has agreed that performance should
begin immediately."*

The waiver buys us nothing on subscriptions. What we get instead is a deduction:
where the consumer asked for performance to start and then withdraws, the provider
may claim compensation for what was supplied, weighing both the time elapsed and
the economic value of what the customer actually consumed.

</OpenQuestion>

## Plan

### Phase 0 — Waiver capture

1. **Mandatory waiver at checkout.** Required to complete the purchase.
   - Detect EU B2C (`is_business_customer` + billing country).
   - *"Give me immediate access. I acknowledge I lose my 14-day withdrawal right
     once I access the content."*
   - Store the **text version + timestamp** on the order. Durable-medium proof is
     itself a legal requirement, and it is what we would produce under challenge.
   - Confirm it back to the customer on the order confirmation email.

2. **An `accessed` predicate per benefit grant.** The grant already carries
   `granted_at` and external identity in `properties`; this normalizes what counts
   as access per type:
   - **Consumption-style** (downloadables, license keys, meter credits): the
     discrete event — download, activation, units consumed.
   - **Provisioned-access** (Discord, GitHub, Slack): the grant *is* the
     supply-begun event, and revoke is real.
   - **Custom and feature flag**: both strategies are grant/revoke no-ops, so
     `granted_at` is all Polar can assert — and it asserts almost nothing. A custom
     benefit may be a thank-you note the customer sees the second checkout
     completes; calling that "accessed" and dropping the refund duty would be
     wrong. **Excluded from the access read.** They fall to *not accessed*, so we
     refund. Merchants are told we cannot see whether the purchase was used.

3. **Order-scoped benefit revocation** so a refund pulls back everything granted
   through that order, and nothing the customer owns from another one.

### Phase 1 — The withdrawal function

Article 11a is prescriptive. A "request a refund" link does not satisfy it.

4. Implement the `refund_request` case type, mirroring `dispute_case.py`: a
   `RefundRequestSupportCase` subclass with a FK to `order`, plus a handler service.
5. Build the **customer-facing case UI** (net-new portal surface). This also
   unblocks customer-visible disputes later — the `customer` audience already
   exists in the model.
6. Build the **withdrawal function** to spec:
   - Labelled "withdraw from contract here" or equivalent.
   - Prominent, easy to find, available for the whole withdrawal period.
   - Collects or confirms the customer's name, which contract, and contact details.
   - A second **"confirm withdrawal"** button after a review screen.
   - Confirmation of receipt by email without undue delay, carrying the content of
     the withdrawal and the timestamp.
   - For a subscription, an explicit **"cancel so I am not charged again"** choice.
     A refund does not imply cancellation, but someone invoking withdrawal usually
     wants both — ask rather than infer.

### Phase 2 — Routing

7. On submission, classify against the three cases above.
   - *Waived + accessed*: open the case, notify the merchant, wait. Subject to
     `organization.can_refund` and `order.refunds_blocked`, which already guard
     `refund/service.py:219`.
   - *Waived + not accessed*: refund immediately, revoke, close the case. No
     merchant approval — the duty is Polar's.
   - *Subscription*: refund with the pro-rata deduction, cancel if asked.
8. Show the merchant **why** on every case: jurisdiction, waiver state, access
   state, days elapsed, and what we did. Where Polar refunded without asking, say
   so plainly rather than letting it look like a silent debit.

### Phase 3 — Abuse controls

9. **Per-customer refund metrics** — derivable today by filtering the refund table
   on `customer_id`; surface "N refund requests across your products."
10. **Customer notes** (net-new field).
11. **Email / card blocklist** — net-new, or lean on Stripe Radar + the existing
    `fraudulent` refund reason (which already warns it blocks customers).

## Always a case

Every withdrawal goes through a `refund_request` case, including the ones Polar
settles without asking anyone. It is cheap — the primitive exists — and the case
history *is* the per-customer abuse signal Phase 3 needs.

## Settled

- **Polar is the trader.** Where the right survives, Polar refunds and debits the
  merchant. The merchant does not get a veto.
- **The waiver is mandatory**, subject to counsel. See the bet.
- **Access is always immediate.** No benefit hold-back, on any path.
- **The 14-day clock starts at order paid** — conclusion of the contract.
- **Revocation is order-scoped**, not per-benefit.
- **Custom and feature flag benefits count as not accessed**, so they refund.
- **Subscriptions ignore the waiver** and refund pro rata.
- **Refund does not imply cancellation.** The customer says so explicitly.
- **The button is not merchant-toggleable.** A merchant cannot opt out of the law.
